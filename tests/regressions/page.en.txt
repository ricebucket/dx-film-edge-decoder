--- intro
Identify a film from the barcode on its edge
Not sure which film a negative or slide is? The answer is on the film itself. Most 35 mm film made since 1983 has a DX barcode along one edge. Copy its bars, or read them from a photo, to find the film, its maker and the frame number.

--- How it works
How the DX edge code works
Kodak introduced DX encoding in 1983. Film for color negative processing carries a latent image barcode along one edge, exposed at the factory and visible after development; on other film it is optional. Lab machines read it to recognize the film and, later, to number the frames.
Two tracks
The clock track on top, nearest the sprocket holes, is the same on every film: a wide mark, a run of thin bars and a shorter wide mark, which machines use to find each cell. The data track below holds the information: each cell is a bar (1) or a gap (0).

Cells Field What it holds
1–6 Start Always 101010, under the wide clock mark.
7–13 Part 1 Product class, a 7-bit number with weights 64, 32, 16, 8, 4, 2, 1.
14 Unassigned Always empty.
15–18 Part 2 Specifier, a 4-bit number with weights 8, 4, 2, 1.
19–24 Frame Frame number, a 6-bit number with weights 32 to 1.
25 A Half-frame flag: a bar means the “A” position between two frames.
26 Unassigned Always empty.
27 Parity Set so that cells 7–25 plus this cell hold an even number of bars.
28–31 Stop Always 0101, under the shorter clock mark.
The DX number
Part 1 and Part 2 together form the film’s DX number, written like 79-15. Part 1 numbers were assigned to manufacturers by their industry association (NAPM, then PIMA, then I3A), and each manufacturer used Part 2 to tell its products apart. The cassette usually carries the same number: the middle four digits of its barcode are 16 × Part 1 + Part 2, so 79-15 becomes 1279. A maker may also add 128, 256 or 384 to Part 1 on the cassette alone, so a few cassettes carry a number that cannot appear on the film edge.
Frame numbers
The 31-cell layout, patented by Kodak in 1990, adds a frame number. The code is printed twice per frame, once at the frame (23) and once halfway to the next (23A), so a notch punched by a printing machine never removes every code. The number printed just before a barcode, to its left when reading, is the number it carries. Before frame 0 the numbers count down: 00 is stored as 63, X as 62 and XX as 61, so Kodak rolls for example run X, XA, 00, 00A, 0.

Edge numbers are exposed at the factory, so they rarely line up exactly with the pictures or a camera’s counter.
Older film
The original code is shorter: 23 cells, with 7 thin bars in the top row instead of 11, and no frame number. Choose 7 thin bars before copying one; otherwise the decoder points this out and offers to switch.
The parity check
The parity cell is a simple guard against misreading: it holds a bar exactly when Part 1, Part 2, the frame number and the A flag contain an odd number of bars, so the total is always even. When a copy fails the check, one bar is probably wrong. The decoder then lists the single-bar corrections that would give a valid code, starting with known films.
Reading direction and film type
Read the code from the wide clock mark. With the film the right way around, usually viewed from the shiny base side, the printed numbers read normally and the wide mark is on the left; from the other side, everything is mirrored. On slide film the bars show clear on a black edge. The code repeats every half frame, so if a bar is unclear, check the next one.
What the code does not say
The DX number names a product. The product name usually includes the speed, but not always. The official list used here dates from 2008, so films introduced later are identified from community reports. Many films are also sold under several names, and the edge code is exposed by the factory that made the film, so one code can belong to a whole family of rebranded films.
