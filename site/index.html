Identify a film from the barcode on its edge

Not sure which film your negatives or slides are? They can tell you themselves. Most 35 mm film made since 1983 has a DX barcode along one edge. Copy its bars, or read them from a photo, to find the film, its maker and the frame number.

  1. Find the barcode

    Look at the strip between the sprocket holes and the edge of the film: two touching rows of small bars, about 13 mm long, repeated twice per frame. A loupe or a zoomed-in scan helps.

  2. Turn the film the right way round

    Hold it so the barcode runs below the sprocket holes and the small printed numbers read normally. The top row should then start with a wide bar on the left. If the wide bar is on the right, turn on Mirror.

  3. Copy the lower row

    Click every cell of the lower row where your film has a bar. Drag to fill several. The top row and both ends are identical on every film, so they are already drawn. Older film may have a shorter code with only 7 thin bars in the top row: choose 7 thin bars under Layout.

Layout
Film

Count the thin bars in the top row between the wide mark at the start and the shorter wide mark at the end. 11 thin bars: the 31-cell layout with a frame number, introduced around 1990. 7 thin bars: the original 23-cell layout from 1983, still used on some film in the 1990s; it identifies the film but carries no frame number.

This only changes how the strip is drawn, so it is easier to compare with your film. On color and black-and-white negatives the bars are dark. On slide film the edge is black and the bars are clear, because reversal processing turns exposed areas clear: treat the clear marks as bars.

Find a film’s code

Search by film name, maker or DX number, then put its barcode on the strip to compare it with your film. Results come from the 2008 I3A registry and the community-run Big Film Database.

Have the cassette?

The six digits printed under the barcode on a 35 mm cassette carry the same DX number as the film edge, plus the roll length. Enter them to identify the film.

How the DX edge code works

Kodak introduced DX encoding in 1983. Besides the checkerboard contacts and barcode on the cassette, every roll carries a latent image barcode exposed along one edge of the film during manufacture. It stays invisible until development, and photofinishing machines read it to recognize the film and, later, to number the frames.

Two tracks

The code has two rows that touch. The clock track on top, nearest the sprocket holes, is the same on every film: a wide mark, a run of thin bars and a shorter wide mark. Machines use it to find each cell. The data track below, along the film edge, holds the information: every cell is either a bar (1) or a gap (0).

The DX number

Part 1 and Part 2 together form the film’s DX number, written like 79-15. Part 1 numbers were assigned to manufacturers by their industry association (NAPM, then PIMA, then I3A), and each manufacturer used Part 2 to tell its products apart. The cassette carries the same number: the middle four digits of its barcode are 16 × Part 1 + Part 2, so 79-15 becomes 1279.

Frame numbers

The 31-cell layout, patented by Kodak in 1990, adds a frame number. The code is printed twice per frame, once at the frame (23) and once halfway to the next (23A), so a notch punched by a printing machine never removes every code. The number printed just before a barcode, to its left when reading, is the number it carries. Before frame 0, Kodak rolls for example run X, XA, 00, 00A; in the barcode these are stored as 62 and 63.

Edge numbers are exposed at the factory, independently of your camera, so they rarely line up exactly with each picture. Use them to tell frames apart rather than to match your camera’s counter. With a half-frame camera, count positions instead: 0, 0A, 1, 1A … are half frames 0, 1, 2, 3 …

Older film

The original code, from before the frame-number extension and still used on some film after it, is shorter: 23 cells, with only 7 thin bars in the top row instead of 11. They carry the film’s DX number but no frame number, so choose 7 thin bars under Layout before copying one. If an old code is copied into the 31-cell strip anyway, it still reads as the right film, but with a meaningless frame number (10 or 42); the decoder points this out and offers to switch.

The parity check

The parity cell is a simple guard against misreading: it holds a bar exactly when Part 1, Part 2, the frame number and the A flag contain an odd number of bars, so the total is always even. When your copy fails the check, one bar is probably wrong. The decoder then lists the single-bar corrections that would give a valid code, starting with known films.

Reading direction and film type

Read the code with the wide clock mark first. When the film is the right way round, usually with its shiny base side toward you, the printed numbers beside the barcode read normally and the wide mark is on the left. Seen from the other side, everything is mirrored. On slide film the edge is black and the bars show as clear marks. Faded or scratched codes are common; since the code repeats every half frame, check the next one along if a bar is unclear.

What the code cannot tell you

The DX number names a product. The product’s name usually includes its speed, but not always. The official list used here dates from 2008, so films introduced later are identified from community reports. Many films are also sold under several names, and the edge code is exposed by the factory that made the film, so one code can belong to a whole family of rebranded films.
