Identify a film from the barcode on its edge

Not sure which film your negatives or slides are? They can tell you themselves. Most 35 mm film made since 1983 has a DX barcode along one edge. Copy its bars, or read them from a photo, to find the film, its maker and the frame number.

  1. Find the barcode

    Two touching rows of small bars between the sprocket holes and the film edge, twice per frame. A loupe helps.

  2. Turn the film the right way around

    Hold it so that it looks like the film strip shown below. Turn on to flip the strip left to right.

  3. Copy the lower row

    Click each cell of the lower row where your film has a bar, or drag across several. The rest is the same on every film and already drawn. If your top row has only 7 thin bars, choose .

Layout

Count the thin bars between the two wide marks of the top row. 11 thin bars: the 31-cell layout with a frame number, from about 1990. 7 thin bars: the original 23-cell layout from 1983, without a frame number.

Have the cassette?

The six digits on a 35 mm cassette usually carry the film’s DX number and the roll length. Enter them to identify the film.

How the DX edge code works

Kodak introduced DX encoding in 1983. Film for color negative processing carries a latent image barcode along one edge, exposed at the factory and visible after development; on other film it is optional. Lab machines read it to recognize the film and, later, to number the frames.

Two tracks

The clock track on top, nearest the sprocket holes, is the same on every film: a wide mark, a run of thin bars and a shorter wide mark, which machines use to find each cell. The data track below holds the information: each cell is a bar (1) or a gap (0).

The DX number

Part 1 and Part 2 together form the film’s DX number, written like 79-15. Part 1 numbers were assigned to manufacturers by their industry association (NAPM, then PIMA, then I3A), and each manufacturer used Part 2 to tell its products apart. The cassette usually carries the same number: the middle four digits of its barcode are 16 × Part 1 + Part 2, so 79-15 becomes 1279. A maker may also add 128, 256 or 384 to Part 1 on the cassette alone, so a few cassettes carry a number that cannot appear on the film edge.

Frame numbers

The 31-cell layout, patented by Kodak in 1990, adds a frame number. The code is printed twice per frame, once at the frame (23) and once halfway to the next (23A), so a notch punched by a printing machine never removes every code. The number printed just before a barcode, to its left when reading, is the number it carries. Before frame 0 the numbers count down: 00 is stored as 63, X as 62 and XX as 61, so Kodak rolls for example run X, XA, 00, 00A, 0.

Edge numbers are exposed at the factory, so they rarely line up exactly with your pictures or your camera’s counter.

Older film

The original code is shorter: 23 cells, with 7 thin bars in the top row instead of 11, and no frame number. Choose before copying one; if you forget, the decoder points this out and offers to switch.

The parity check

The parity cell is a simple guard against misreading: it holds a bar exactly when Part 1, Part 2, the frame number and the A flag contain an odd number of bars, so the total is always even. When your copy fails the check, one bar is probably wrong. The decoder then lists the single-bar corrections that would give a valid code, starting with known films.

Reading direction and film type

Read the code from the wide clock mark. With the film the right way around, usually shiny base side toward you, the printed numbers read normally and the wide mark is on the left; from the other side, everything is mirrored. On slide film the bars show clear on a black edge. The code repeats every half frame, so if a bar is unclear, check the next one.

What the code cannot tell you

The DX number names a product. The product name usually includes the speed, but not always. The official list used here dates from 2008, so films introduced later are identified from community reports. Many films are also sold under several names, and the edge code is exposed by the factory that made the film, so one code can belong to a whole family of rebranded films.
